The hall smelled of rosin and cold stone and wet wool. Alda led the final, four touches to three. One more and the purse was hers — the winter's coal and her sister's medicine.

Vollmer was better than her. That was the first true thing, and she had stopped arguing with it in October. He was twenty-four, his wrist moved like water, and the gallery had begun to say his name between touches the way a crowd does when it is choosing a favorite.

But she had watched him. Not the way a rival watches, with envy, but the way a carpenter watches a hinge. Whenever she parried in quarte — her father's parry, her father's blade — he beat once, feinted inside, and finished low, under her arm. Twice in October. Twice tonight. The man had one beautiful idea, and it worked every time.

A straight exchange would not win this. It had not won in October.

So on the last touch she did not parry.

He came in, saw her blade hanging a fraction wide, and took the invitation the way a hungry man takes bread. Beat. Feint. His point dropped for the low line, exactly as it had all season.

She stepped in.

He saw her do it — she felt him see it — and his wrist began to turn, a finger's width too late. Her front foot came down beside his. Her blade slid over his and carried it past her hip. Her own point came up and touched him under the collarbone, light as a fingertip.

The blade rang wrong. A flat, splitting note. She felt something give above the guard, and she finished anyway.

The judge called the touch. Vollmer stood still, then laughed — one short, disbelieving sound — and offered his hand.

She took it. Her grip shook when she let go.

Only then did she look at the blade. When she lowered it, the blade came free of the guard and struck the stone at her feet. She had rewrapped that grip herself in October, with the last of the waxed cord from his bench.

She crouched, gathered both pieces, and went to collect the purse.
