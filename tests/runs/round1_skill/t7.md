"The clasp gave," Mr. Vance said. "The stone fell behind the radiator. That's what I'll tell Mrs. Aldrich."

Tomas stayed by the door, one hand still on the latch. The ring lay in its open box on the cutting table, midway between them.

"She'll want to see the setting."

"She'll want to see me." Vance trimmed the wick. "You'll pour her tea and keep your eyes on the tray."

"It was for my sister. She's—"

"I didn't ask." Vance turned a page without reading it. "Three years, not two. Wages as they are. You'll sleep upstairs, and the neighbors will see you come down at six, the way you always have. On Thursday you'll say the words to her yourself, once, and then go finish the hemming."

He set the brandy down at Tomas's elbow. Tomas did not touch it.

Mrs. Aldrich kept the largest account in the book. The letter from Bristol was folded in Tomas's coat. Vance had not mentioned it.

"It's unlocked," Vance said. "If you go, the story belongs to whoever tells it first."

Tomas looked at the door, then at the box on the table, open and empty of everything but velvet.

Vance slid the key across the wood. "Lock up."

Tomas took it, crossed the room, and turned the bolt. The lock went over with a sound like a swallow.

Behind him, Vance had already written the line himself, in his own hand — Clasp faulty, stone lost — and was blotting it once, then again, until the second blot went through the page.

"Thursday, then," Vance said.

"Thursday," Tomas said. He put the key in his pocket. It was still warm.
