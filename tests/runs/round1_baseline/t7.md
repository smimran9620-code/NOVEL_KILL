The room smelled of cold ash. Voss set the lamp between them and slid the file across the table.

"Sign it," Ada said.

He didn't. He opened the file and read the charge aloud again — arson, six counts, the names of the dead — while she stood with her wrists still marked from the rope. Then, slowly, he fed the first page into the lamp flame.

Ada watched the corner curl. She didn't breathe. The flame ate the names first, then the margins.

He let it burn to his fingers, dropped it, and ground it under his boot. "There. No trial."

"Then I'm free."

"Sit down."

She didn't. He didn't move to stop her, either, and that was somehow worse.

He took the second copy from his coat and laid it on the table, unlit. "This one stays in the drawer. The governor's seal is on it. It's dated tomorrow, right after the trial that isn't going to happen." He smoothed the paper flat with two fingers. "If I die, it gets filed. If I lose the county, it gets filed. If you run, it gets filed."

"There's snow on the pass," she said. "It's closed till spring."

"I know."

He wiped the ash from his fingers on a cloth, folded it twice, and set it down neatly beside the lamp. Outside, wind worried the shutter. She could still smell the burnt paper.

Ada looked at the drawer. Then at his hand.

"Say thank you," he said, not unkindly, "and I'll put it away."

"Thank you," she said.

He unlocked the drawer, put the paper inside, turned the key twice, and dropped it into his pocket. Then he poured them both a drink. She took hers with both hands.
