The gavel fell at nine thousand, and Ilse kept her hands flat on the table because they had begun to shake.

Corvane didn't look at her. He never did when he lost; he looked at the ceiling, as if the plaster had cheated him. Around them the auction room exhaled: two clerks, a banker, the widow Frome fanning herself with her catalogue. Corvane had taken the Kessler lease and the north wharf with that same untroubled face. Ilse had counted them all that morning.

Three weeks in the records office had taught her what the catalogue would not. The eastern lot carried a reversion clause: raise the seawall within two years or forfeit the ground to the city. Corvane, who bought paper and never read footnotes, would not have found it. But a trap wasn't a victory. She had needed his number, too.

She got it from his clerk in the lobby at eight. *Nine thousand, and not a penny past it. He said so himself.* A man who announces his ceiling has already built it into the room.

The bidding went slowly, then quickly. Six. Seven. Seven-five from the widow. Corvane's hand lifted without hurry; Ilse matched him, matched him again, and at eight-five she caught the pause, half a breath, his thumb stopping on the catalogue's edge, and knew the clerk had told the truth.

"Nine," she said.

Corvane's fingers tapped the rail once. He did not bid.

She had won. She sat in the damp wool of her coat while the room applauded politely and understood, in the same breath, what it had cost. The money was not hers. It was the shop, mortgaged to Hale at a rate that would eat her by spring; it was her mother's rings, folded into a pawn ticket in her purse; it was her sister's dowry, promised and now gone. All of it, on one lot by the river.

Corvane rose and buttoned his coat. "You'll want a surveyor," he said, almost kindly. "The tithe map is wrong."

She had the tithe map. She had the clause. What she did not have was two years and eleven thousand more for the seawall, or a shop left to borrow against.

"Thank you," Ilse said, and folded the deed into her coat, and walked out into the rain before he could see her hands shake.
