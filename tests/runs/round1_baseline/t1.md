The plot runs on coincidence and unearned knowledge, not causality.

The map is the first gap: an unidentified stranger supplies exactly the route Wren needs, with no motive, and she trusts it unverified. Oris's turn is thin: shared food shows decency, not readiness to commit treason; kindness to collaboration needs a personal stake.

Security logic is unclear: an intruder and one guard reach the throne room during a surrender signing, yet no guard or aide intervenes. The treaty's timing merely positions Wren for the climax.

The army is the largest break: it arrives "because the army had been waiting for a signal," but no signal is sent, no sender identified, and its arrival coincides with Wren's argument. Its position is incoherent—at the gates yet surrounding the palace—and an army at the gates cannot stop a signing inside; the chancellor's panic and attack are asserted, not caused.

The resolution is doubly unearned: Wren "remembers" an allergy never planted, and a thrown silver inkwell causes collapse—medically implausible, reliant on a convenient object. Even if the chancellor falls, someone else could sign; stopping the treaty needs political or legal consequence, not merely an incapacitated signatory.

Oris, the stranger, and the army all vanish from the resolution. The allergy causes the climax; the army is a false climax, and the plots never connect. Establish who sends the map and signal, give Oris a motive, plant the allergy, and make the treaty's defeat follow from Wren's actions and political forces.
