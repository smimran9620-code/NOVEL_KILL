# Validation Report

This report records what was actually run, what happened, what was revised, and what the results do and do not support. Raw outputs are in tests/runs/ with the blinding key in tests/runs/blinding_key.json.

## Method

- **Seven tasks**, none derived from the two reference novels: plot-logic diagnosis (t1), strategic scene drafting (t3), five-chapter causal grid (t4), continuity audit (t6), theme-through-action scene (t7), style-preserving line revision (t9), and a false-positive control on a working scene (t10). Full prompts and criteria are in evaluation_prompts.md.
- **Two conditions:** task prompt alone (baseline), and task plus the skill (skill-guided).
- **One output per cell**, generated by the same underlying model, 150-400 words per task.
- **Blind judging:** outputs were anonymised and randomised per test. The judge did not know the conditions, scored five criteria 0-3, and chose a winner. Judges were told to ignore self-descriptions of process.
- **Three rounds:** round 1 tested the skill as first written (all seven tasks). Round 2 retested the four tasks the skill lost, after targeted revisions. Round 3 retested t1 once more after a second, narrower revision.

Limits are stated in section 5. This is a small, exploratory evaluation, not a controlled experiment.

**Round 1 mean:** baseline 13.29/15; skill 14.00/15. **Head-to-head:** baseline won 4, skill won 3.

The honest reading of round 1 is that the skill did not demonstrate superiority. It improved drafting and line revision, and it hurt short diagnostic tasks by adding process overhead and by making fixes less concrete.

## Diagnosis of the four failures, and the revisions

The judges' reasons pointed at three specific defects, not general weakness:

1. **Repairs were directions, not instructions.** In t1 the skill named faults well and then wrote "repair starts with the army and the map: one chain, planted, then paid" — no cause, no placement, no effect. Baseline was more actionable.
   *Revision:* a Fix Specification rule. Every fix must state (a) the cause to plant, (b) where it is planted relative to the fault, (c) what changes as a result. One mechanism per fix. Alternatives go in a separate "Options" line.
2. **Continuity fixes hedged.** In t6 the skill wrote "a relief column arrived, or they wear garrison colors" and offered a travel-time fix ("the fort's estimate counted laden wagons") that was renumbering dressed as story.
   *Revision:* a continuity resolution protocol with exactly three legitimate moves (earliest wins, dramatise the correction, retire the old fact) and an explicit test: does the fix change what a character can do, or only what the text asserts?
3. **Working scenes were over-diagnosed.** In t10 the skill added a continuity nit and a decorative-detail complaint to a scene that worked.
   *Revision:* when a scene largely works, cap findings at two, label them minor, and do not require new setup for decorative detail.
4. **Grids did not close on the opening.** In t4 the baseline's final chapter reused chapter one's flaw; the skill's did not.
   *Revision:* a closure-by-reuse rule. The climax must reuse at least one concrete element from the first chapter or first arc, with changed meaning.

Two of four losses became wins, and one became a tie. t1 regressed for a new reason: "do not rewrite it" was read as "do not propose repairs". The Fix Specification rule had been written, but the no-rewrite instruction overrode it.

## What the results support

- The skill **improved drafting of strategic scenes and thematic scenes** (t3, t7): the outputs showed preparation visible before payoff, competent opponents, and thematic control carried by action rather than statement.
- The skill **improved style-preserving revision** (t9) by a wide margin, mainly by refusing to literary-ise a pulpy passage while still fixing rhythm and blocking.
- The skill **improved diagnosis once repair specificity and no-over-diagnosis rules were added** (t1, t10).
- The skill **improved continuity repair** when forced to choose one mechanism and to make the fix change what characters can do (t6).

## What the results do not support

- **Superiority in general.** Round 1, the only round uncontaminated by seeing scores, was 3-4 against the baseline with a small mean advantage. The later wins came after revisions made in response to those scores. That is normal product iteration, but it is not evidence of general superiority.
- **Architecture gains.** t4 tied twice. Planning tasks may benefit less because the baseline model is already competent at five-beat causality, and because the skill's value there is in checks, not in generated content.
- **Any claim about human readers.** All judges were the same model family. No human editors or readers rated the outputs.
- **Any claim about long manuscripts.** Every task was under 400 words. The skill's continuity and long-form rules were not exercised at scale.

## Threats to validity

- **Small n.** One output per cell; differences of one point are noise. Judge variance is visible: the same baseline t1 output scored 14 in round 1 and 11 in round 3, a three-point swing with no change to the text. Treat single-digit differences as unstable.
- **Multiple revisions.** Three rounds of editing were made in response to scores; a fourth round would probably produce further gains purely by iteration.
- **Same-family judges.** The judge shares the generator's tendencies, including a possible preference for structured, labelled output.
- **Process leakage.** Some skill-guided outputs named their mode or cited failure codes. Judges were told to ignore process talk, but formatting may still have signalled guidance.
- **Author bias.** The tasks, criteria, and rubric were written by the same author as the skill; they may favour the skill's strengths.
- **Revision contamination.** Rounds 2 and 3 were designed after seeing round 1 scores, so they test the revised skill, not an independent hypothesis.
- **No human baseline.** "Better" here means "scored higher by a model judge against explicit criteria", not "better received by readers".
- **Short forms only.** Nothing here validates novel-length use.

## Recommended next validation

1. Human blind rating by 3-5 editors on 10 paired outputs, with inter-rater agreement reported.
2. A long-form test: plan and draft three consecutive chapters (6,000+ words) with and without the skill; measure causal-link density, scene-type variety, continuity errors, and repetition.
3. A revision test on a real 3,000-word chapter with planted continuity, causality, and voice problems; measure detection recall, false positives, and voice preservation.
4. A cost test: tokens and time per task, since the skill's process overhead is a real cost.
5. A cross-model test (different model families as both writer and judge).

## Bottom line

The skill is a useful instrument with measurable, task-specific benefits in drafting, thematic control, style-preserving revision, and structured diagnosis after revision. It is not proven superior to an unaided strong model, and the evidence is too small and too self-referential to claim otherwise. Its most defensible claim is narrower: it makes certain craft failures easier to see and certain repairs more concrete, and it does so without flattening voice in the one test designed to detect flattening.
