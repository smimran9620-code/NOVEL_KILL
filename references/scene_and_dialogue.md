# Scene and Dialogue Technique

Expansion of SKILL.md Stage 5, for when a draft must be built or repaired at scene level.

## 1. The scene as a pressure system

A scene is a bounded unit in which someone wants something, something resists, and something changes. Four elements must be present, even in a quiet scene:

- **Want.** Concrete and immediate. "She wants him to admit he lied", not "she wants closure".
- **Resistance.** A person with a conflicting want, a rule, a shortage, a deadline, or the POV's own divided motive.
- **Turn.** The moment the terms change: new information, a refusal, a concession, a price revealed.
- **Consequence.** What the turn makes necessary next.

If any element is missing, the scene is either a summary (no want, no turn) or a mood piece (no consequence).

## 2. Enter late, leave early

Begin after the greetings. Begin at the moment the pressure starts. End at the moment the consequence is clear, not after the characters have discussed it.

Bad entry: arrival, weather, seating, small talk.
Good entry: the first sentence that could not be said in any other scene.

Bad exit: summary of what was decided, plus a reflective paragraph.
Good exit: the decision stated or implied, and the cost visible.

## 3. Tactic escalation

Characters rarely do their best move first. Give them three attempts, each worse or more costly:

1. Social tactic (ask, charm, invoke status).
2. Transactional tactic (trade, threaten, bargain).
3. Costly tactic (reveal a secret, accept a wound, break a rule).

The escalation is the scene's rhythm. A scene where the first tactic works is a transition; keep it short.

## 4. Dialogue that negotiates

Each speaker enters with an objective. The exchange is a negotiation over who controls the frame.

Techniques:

- **Answer a different question.** Deflection reveals what the character protects.
- **Concede a small thing to protect a large one.** The reader sees the priority.
- **Change the subject as pressure.** What is avoided is information.
- **Use a concrete detail as a weapon.** "You still keep two cups" says more than an accusation.
- **Let silence do work.** A non-answer after a direct question is a line of dialogue.

Avoid:

- characters explaining the world to each other;
- characters stating their own psychology accurately;
- everyone speaking in the same syntax;
- tag inflation ("he said angrily, furiously") in place of action.

## 5. Interiority without stalling

Interiority is useful when it changes the meaning of an action, not when it repeats the action.

Use one of these after the action, not before:

- an inference the POV draws from a detail;
- a memory triggered by a concrete cue;
- a calculation of cost;
- a lie the POV tells themselves, visible to the reader.

Keep it short at high pressure. Long reflection belongs in the aftermath, and even there it should be interrupted by a practical demand.

## 6. Description as constraint

Describe what the POV would notice given their goal and history, and prefer details that constrain action: the door that sticks, the mud, the smell that gives away a hiding place, the cost of the coat.

One precise detail per paragraph beats a catalogue. A catalogue is acceptable only when the POV is deliberately overwhelmed, and then it should be shaped by their panic or greed.

## 7. Action scenes

- Establish the space and the stakes before the first blow.
- Give the reader a plan, then break it. A fight without a plan is noise.
- Alternate wide and close: the tactical situation, then one concrete sensation.
- Damage must persist. Injuries, lost tools, and expended resources carry into later scenes.
- End on a consequence, not on the last punch.

## 8. Quiet scenes

Quiet scenes carry the novel's emotional accounting. They need the same structure, with lower stakes:

- a want that is small but real (to be forgiven, to avoid a topic, to be seen);
- resistance from another person's ordinary self-interest;
- a turn that changes the relationship by one degree;
- a consequence that shows in the next scene.

## 9. Pacing inside a scene

Vary sentence length with pressure. Use paragraph breaks as beats. A one-line paragraph is a held breath; three in a row is melodrama. Let the reader's eye slow at the turn.

## 10. Revision pass for scenes

1. Write the change statement. If it is "nothing", cut or merge.
2. Identify the turn. If it is caused by coincidence, plant a cause.
3. Check the tactic escalation; flatten if all three attempts are identical.
4. Read dialogue aloud; cut any line that explains what the reader already knows.
5. Delete the first paragraph and the last paragraph; if the scene still works, keep them deleted.
6. Check the consequence is used in the next scene.
