# Craft Principles: A Causal Model of Narrative Quality

This file explains why the rules in SKILL.md exist. Use it when planning, when teaching, or when a user asks "why does that matter?" It is deliberately organised as mechanisms, not as a list of virtues.

## 1. What quality means, operationally

A reader keeps reading when three quantities are simultaneously positive:

- **An open question.** Something is unresolved and the reader wants the resolution. Curiosity is cheap to create and easy to exhaust.
- **Felt stakes.** The resolution matters to someone the reader is invested in, and the investment was earned by witnessed cost.
- **Trust.** The reader believes the resolution will be possible, fair, and meaningful, because the text has been consistent and has paid small promises before.

Quality is therefore not "beautiful sentences". It is a set of causal relationships between text and reader:

| Reader experience | Textual cause |
| --- | --- |
| Curiosity | A question the text makes salient and delays |
| Suspense | A question whose answer depends on a character's choice, with costs on both sides |
| Surprise | An outcome that contradicts the reader's model but follows from information already given |
| Satisfaction | A promise made early and paid exactly once, with the payment changing the meaning of the promise |
| Belief | Decisions reconstructable from motive and available information |
| Emotional weight | Accumulated cost, not stated emotion |
| Immersion | Specific, consistent sensory and social detail that constrains action |

Everything below is an application of this table.

## 2. The five chains

### Chain 1: Desire -> obstacle -> choice -> consequence

This is the scene engine, and it is recursive: the consequence of one scene becomes the obstacle or opportunity of the next.

Why it works: a choice under pressure reveals character more efficiently than description, because the reader infers the motive from the cost the character accepts. Consequence converts the choice into plot.

Failure signature: the character reacts, observes, or is moved by others. The scene ends with the situation unchanged; the next scene could have begun without it.

Test: delete the scene. If the next scene is still possible with the same meaning, the scene was texture.

### Chain 2: Constraint -> cost -> strategy

Rules generate story only through costs. A magic system that lets you do anything is a decoration; one that charges a price creates strategy, because strategy is the art of spending limited resources well.

Why it works: once a cost is legible, the reader can evaluate decisions. Evaluation is engagement. Without costs, victories are unearned and defeats are arbitrary.

Failure signature: powers appear when needed, scale inflates without new constraints, and the reader stops doing the arithmetic because the arithmetic no longer predicts anything.

Test: name what the last victory cost, who paid, and who now wants revenge or repayment. If nothing, the victory is a set piece.

### Chain 3: Expectation -> delay -> fulfillment / complication / overturn

Setups are promises. They can be kept, complicated (the thing arrives but means something else), or overturned (the expectation was based on a false premise), but they must be addressed.

Why it works: the reader's model of the story is a set of expectations; manipulating them is the basic pleasure of plot. Unaddressed expectations are experienced as authorial carelessness, even when the author forgot rather than cheated.

Failure signature: mysteries without answers, loaded objects that never fire, prophecies that neither come true nor are meaningfully defied.

Test: maintain a register. Every entry must have an answer, a payoff chapter, and the earliest fair clue.

### Chain 4: Information asymmetry -> inference -> inevitable surprise

The strongest surprises are retroactively necessary. The reader had the pieces; the arrangement was hidden.

Why it works: it rewards attention, which builds trust, and it makes the reveal feel authored rather than imposed.

Failure signature: the reveal depends on information withheld from the reader entirely, or on a character acting out of character to conceal it.

Test: after the reveal, can the reader find two earlier scenes where the truth was visible without being labelled? If not, the reveal is a trick.

### Chain 5: State change per unit

Every chapter and scene should change at least one of: knowledge, relationship, stakes, possession, position, or self-understanding.

Why it works: change is what the reader tracks. A unit with no change is a report; reports are only tolerable as transitions and only when short.

Failure signature: chapters that restate the situation, repeat a prior argument, or deliver information the reader already had.

Test: write the change statement in one sentence. If it is "nothing", cut, merge, or convert to summary inside another unit.

## 3. Comparative dimension table

Fifteen dimensions. Each row states what "working" looks like, the failure signature, and the trade-off that prevents a simple good/bad judgment.

| Dimension | Working | Failure signature | Trade-off and exception |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Premise | Generates conflicts without new inventions | Needs constant new elements to stay interesting | High-concept premises can be thin if execution is precise; quiet premises need deeper psychology |
| Causality | Events follow from prior decisions and consequences | Events follow from authorial need | Coincidence is acceptable as an inciting incident or complication, not as a resolution |
| Character agency | Characters change the situation | The plot changes the characters | Reactive protagonists can work in horror or tragedy if the reaction is the drama |
| Psychology | Decisions reconstructable from motive and information | Decisions convenient for the plot | Opacity is acceptable if the reader is given a later, fair explanation |
| Originality | Familiar elements transformed into specific ones | Familiar elements merely assembled | Genre furniture is not a defect; unexamined furniture is |
| Worldbuilding | Rules generate costs, conflict, and institutions | Rules are exposition and decoration | Soft magic can work if cost is emotional or moral rather than mechanical |
| Pacing | Each section earns its place by change | Sections repeat or stall | Slow pacing is a choice; it must be filled with pressure, not information |
| Scene design | Goal, obstacle, turn, decision, consequence | Scene reports rather than dramatises | Summary is correct for transitions and for deliberate tempo |
| Dialogue | Reveals character, negotiates power, advances conflict | Explains what both parties know | Stylised dialogue may forgo realism for rhythm; it still needs function |
| Prose | Precise, rhythmic, suited to moment | Generic, abstract, uniform | Plain prose is a valid instrument; vagueness is not |
| Continuity | Facts preserved or deliberately revised | Facts forgotten or silently changed | Unreliable narration is a deliberate exception, flagged by another source |
| Payoff | Revelations emerge from established material | Revelations arrive from outside | Open endings are valid if the openness is the answer to the question |
| Thematic depth | Ideas emerge through conflict and consequence | Characters speak the theme | Thesis fiction can work if the argument is dramatised as competing goods |
| Long-form sustainability | The engine varies its unit while deepening stakes | The same scene shape repeats with new nouns | Serial ritual can be pleasurable; then vary stakes, not structure |
| Reader engagement | Curiosity plus stakes plus trust | Confusion, indifference, or manipulation | Confusion can be deliberate; it must be bounded and resolved |

## 4. Mechanism library

### 4.1 Dramatic irony

Mechanism: the reader knows something a character does not; every line the character speaks acquires a second meaning. It converts exposition into tension because the reader is waiting for the collision.

Application: place the reader's knowledge one beat ahead of the character's, not ten. Sustain it by letting the character act competently on false premises.

Failure: irony that the text then explains, or that lasts so long the reader forgets the truth.

### 4.2 Setup and payoff

Mechanism: a detail is planted in a context where it reads as texture; later it becomes causal. The reader's memory is rewarded, and the earlier scene is retroactively denser.

Application: plant with a concrete sensory or practical reason to be there. Pay off by changing the meaning of the earlier moment, not merely by using the object.

Failure: plants that announce themselves ("little did he know"), or payoffs that merely confirm what was obvious.

### 4.3 Fair-play surprise

Mechanism: hide the arrangement, not the pieces. The reader can reconstruct after the fact.

Application: write the reveal, then walk backwards and place at least two unobtrusive sightings of the truth. Check that a careful reader could have assembled it.

Failure: withholding information the POV logically possesses; making a character stupid only to preserve the surprise.

### 4.4 Cost as characterisation

Mechanism: what a person will pay, and for what, is their character. Stated traits are claims; costs are evidence.

Application: for every major trait, design one scene where the trait is expensive and the character pays anyway.

Failure: traits asserted by narrator or by other characters, never tested.

### 4.5 Escalation by constraint

Mechanism: raising stakes by narrowing options is more sustainable than raising power levels. A character with more power but fewer choices is in more danger, not less.

Application: each arc adds a constraint (obligation, exposure, time, identity, dependence) and removes an option.

Failure: escalation by number. Each new magnitude cancels the previous one and the reader stops believing in danger.

### 4.6 Variation of scene types

Mechanism: the same emotional beat delivered through a different scene shape feels new. Monotony is structural, not lexical.

Application: track the last five scene types; force a different one. Negotiation, pursuit, ritual, discovery, failure, aftermath, preparation, performance, interrogation, gift.

Failure: every chapter is "protagonist observes, is told something, reflects".

### 4.7 Institutional plot generation

Mechanism: institutions with their own goals produce events without the protagonist. The world seems to move when the POV is absent.

Application: give each institution a resource, a method, and an internal faction. Let their plans mature off-page and arrive as consequences.

Failure: institutions exist only to oppose or reward the protagonist.

### 4.8 Subtext in dialogue

Mechanism: characters rarely say exactly what they mean when stakes are high; the gap is the drama. The reader infers intent from evasion, topic changes, and what is not answered.

Application: give each speaker an objective that conflicts with the other's; let them pursue it indirectly. The reader should be able to translate the subtext but never see it stated.

Failure: characters explain their feelings and the world to each other for the reader's benefit.

### 4.9 Concrete specificity

Mechanism: specificity creates belief and memory. "A knife" is forgettable; "a boning knife with a taped handle" implies a life.

Application: one precise detail per paragraph of description, chosen to constrain action or reveal character; resist catalogues.

Failure: abstraction stacked on abstraction ("a sense of immense power"), which forces the reader to supply the image.

### 4.10 Scene-to-summary ratio

Mechanism: scenes create experience; summaries create orientation. Too much summary and the novel becomes a report; too little and the reader drowns in irrelevant time.

Application: summarise what the reader needs to know but need not feel. Dramatise what later choices depend on.

Failure: summarising the climax; dramatising the commute.

### 4.11 Rhythmic control

Mechanism: sentence length is a pacing instrument. Short sentences accelerate and isolate; long sentences accumulate and implicate.

Application: vary deliberately at turns and decisions. Read aloud. A uniform mid-length hum is the most common AI tell.

Failure: every paragraph the same length and cadence, regardless of pressure.

### 4.12 Emotional accrual

Mechanism: emotion is a balance built by prior cost, not a label applied at the moment. A death matters if the reader has watched the relationship cost something.

Application: invest before you spend. Let the reader see the relationship's ordinary texture and its price. Then the loss lands.

Failure: "she felt immense sadness" after two paragraphs of acquaintance.

### 4.13 Theme through consequence

Mechanism: an idea becomes dramatic when it forces a choice between goods. The character's choice, and the price, is the argument.

Application: design two characters who embody rival answers to the theme's question, and a plot that makes both pay. Do not let either be simply right.

Failure: a mouthpiece speech that the plot then confirms; the reader feels lectured and the idea feels weightless.

## 5. Where the rules bend

A rule is a default with a reason. Break it when you can state the reason and the compensating effect.

- **Telling** works when the information is trivial, when speed matters more than immersion, or when the narrator's voice is itself the pleasure.
- **Repetition** works as refrain, ritual, or comic escalation, when each repetition changes the meaning or the stakes.
- **Coincidence** works as an inciting incident or a complication that forces a choice; it fails when it resolves one.
- **Deus ex machina** works in declared modes (myth, comedy, satire) if the text has taught the reader to expect intervention as a theme; it fails when it solves a problem the story promised to solve through character.
- **Flat or plain prose** works as transparency and speed; it fails when it becomes vagueness.
- **Open endings** work when the unresolved question is the point and the reader has been prepared; they fail when they merely stop.
- **Unreliable narration** works when the text supplies a second source of truth; it fails when it is used to excuse continuity errors.

## 6. Diagnostic decision tree

When a draft feels wrong, locate the level before changing words.

1. Can you state what the protagonist wants in this chapter? If not, the problem is L2/L3 (chapter or arc intent), not prose.
2. Does the chapter change anything? If not, check scene change statements. If they are all "nothing", the problem is L1.
3. Does the change follow from a choice? If not, the problem is causality and agency (L2).
4. Would a reasonable reader have predicted the outcome for the wrong reasons? If yes, the problem is information order (L2) or fair-play (L3).
5. Do the sentences feel uniform? Only now is it L0 prose.
6. Does the ending resolve the question the opening asked? If not, the problem is L4.
