# Weak vs Strong: Original Illustrative Contrasts

All examples are original. They are deliberately small: each pair isolates one decision so the mechanism is visible. Read the mechanism note, not only the text.

## 1. Summary where the story needs a scene

**Weak.**
> The negotiations dragged on for three weeks. Mara eventually got the water rights and the northern clans agreed to the border. She was relieved and returned home.

**Why it fails.** The clause "eventually got" hides the only interesting thing: how. The reader is told the outcome and told the feeling. Nothing is dramatised, so the outcome costs the reader nothing and later consequences have no weight.

**Strong.**
> On the eleventh day Mara put the map face down.
> "You can have the northern road," she said. "Not the spring."
> Old Keshi did not look at the map. "Then you can have the spring," he said, "and no road to reach it."
> She had expected that. She had not expected him to smile while saying it. It meant he had already sold the road to someone else, and the someone had already promised him soldiers.
> "Two days," she said. "I will bring you a better offer."
> She did not have one. She had forty-eight hours and a single favour left.

**Why it works.** The scene has a goal (keep the spring), an obstacle (Keshi's counter), a turn (the smile reveals a prior sale), and a costly decision (buy time with a favour she cannot afford). The reader now understands the political geometry without a paragraph of explanation, and the next scene inherits an urgent constraint.

**Rule.** Dramatise what later choices depend on; summarise what only orients.

## 2. Intelligence asserted vs intelligence demonstrated

**Weak.**
> Kestrel was the smartest thief in the city. He had never been caught, and everyone said no lock could stop him. He looked at the vault and smiled. "Child's play," he said, and opened it.

**Why it fails.** The text asserts competence and then removes the problem without showing the reasoning. The reader has nothing to admire; they are asked to admire.

**Strong.**
> The vault door was a Lister, which meant the tumblers were linked. Kestrel did not touch the dial. He set a wedge under the frame, went to the wall, and knocked twice at knee height; the panel was hollow, as the builder's mark suggested. Inside, the linkage rod ran to a floor bolt.
> He could not pick the lock. He could remove the reason the lock mattered.
> It took him nine minutes and cost him the wedge, which was his father's. When the bolt dropped, the door swung open on its own weight.
> He stood in the doorway a moment longer than he needed to.

**Why it works.** The intelligence is in the inference (linked tumblers imply a linkage), the method (attack the frame, not the lock), and the cost (the father's wedge). A knowledgeable reader can check the logic; a lay reader can follow it. The last line converts competence into character without explaining it.

**Rule.** Show preparation and inference; let the opponent be competent; charge a price.

## 3. Exposition as lecture vs exposition as consequence

**Weak.**
> The city of Vel was governed by the Salt Charter, a law passed three hundred years ago which decreed that only registered merchants could trade in salt. This law had shaped the city's politics ever since, creating a powerful merchant class and a poor underclass.

**Why it fails.** Nothing is at stake in the paragraph. The information is correct and inert; it would fit any worldbuilding wiki.

**Strong.**
> The inspector counted the sacks twice, then asked for her licence.
> "I have one," Sena said, and it was true; it was her brother's, and it had his name on it.
> The inspector looked at the name. Looked at her. "Your brother is dead," he said. "I sold him the salt that killed him."
> Behind him, the weigh-house door was closing. Half her stock was already inside, and the Charter said unlicensed goods inside the weigh-house belonged to the city.
> She had one move. It would cost her the name she had been using for six years.

**Why it works.** The Charter arrives as a trap that forces a choice: the rule is the obstacle, the cost is identity, and the reader learns how the economy works by watching it bite. The information is now memorable because it hurt someone.

**Rule.** Information matters when it blocks an action or sets a price.

## 4. Escalation by number vs escalation by constraint

**Weak.**
> The enemy general had ten thousand men. Then the king sent twenty thousand. Then the demons arrived with a hundred thousand. The war grew larger than anyone had imagined.

**Why it fails.** Numbers do not change what the protagonist can do. Each escalation cancels the previous one; the reader stops doing arithmetic because the arithmetic predicts nothing.

**Strong.**
> The enemy had ten thousand men and one bridge.
> When the king sent twenty thousand, the bridge did not get wider. It got more crowded.
> By the third week the reinforcements were eating the horses. By the fourth, the general had to choose which of his own regiments would hold the ford and which would watch the bridge burn.
> He chose the regiment whose colonel had married his daughter. He told himself it was strategy.

**Why it works.** More soldiers create a bottleneck, a supply crisis, and a moral choice. The escalation is real because it narrows options and raises cost. The last line converts a tactical decision into character.

**Rule.** Escalate by removing options and raising costs, not by adding zeros.

## 5. Philosophy as speech vs philosophy as dilemma

**Weak.**
> "You must understand," the judge said, "that justice is not the same as law. Law is a tool; justice is a principle. A society that confuses the two will destroy itself."

**Why it fails.** The idea is stated by an authority and confirmed by nothing. Delete the speech and the scene loses no meaning.

**Strong.**
> The law was clear: the boy had stolen bread, and the penalty was his hand.
> The judge had written the law. He had written it after his own daughter starved in a winter when the granaries were full.
> "The court finds the accused guilty," he said.
> The boy's mother did not scream. She simply looked at the judge's hands on the bench, and he understood that she knew who had written it.
> He could not change the verdict. He could change the sentence, and everyone in the room would know why.
> "Fine," he said. "The fine is three days of labour at the granary. He will be paid for it."
> The clerk wrote it down. The room did not applaud. The law had just become something it had not been an hour before, and the judge had done it with a number.

**Why it works.** The theme (law vs justice) is tested by a man who must choose between two goods he believes in, and the choice costs him the coherence of his own life's work. No one explains the theme; the reader infers it from the sentence.

**Rule.** Convert the idea into a choice between goods; make both sides pay.

## 6. Unearned twist vs fair-play surprise

**Weak.**
> "I was the traitor all along," the mentor said, and laughed. "Everything you know was a lie." It turned out he had been working for the enemy since the beginning, and no one had ever suspected.

**Why it fails.** The reveal adds information and subtracts trust, but nothing earlier changes meaning. It depends on total concealment, which is not surprise; it is withholding.

**Strong.**
> "You knew about the ford," she said. "You sent us there."
> He did not deny it. He set down the cup, and she remembered the gesture from the first night, when he had set down a cup in exactly that way before telling her the road was safe.
> "I sent you where I needed you," he said. "The ford was the only place the enemy would commit."
> "They died."
> "Yes."
> The terrible thing was not that he had lied. It was that he had told her the truth that first night, in the only words she had not thought to weigh.

**Why it works.** The clue was in plain sight: a repeated gesture tied to a statement she trusted. Rereading changes the first scene's meaning from kindness to calculation. The reveal is both surprising and necessary.

**Rule.** Hide the arrangement, not the pieces. Make the reveal reinterpret a scene the reader already has.

## 7. Emotion labelled vs emotion accrued

**Weak.**
> At the funeral, Ilan felt overwhelming grief. He had loved his brother more than anything, and now he was gone forever. Tears streamed down his face as he thought about all the good times they had shared.

**Why it fails.** The reader is handed the conclusion. "All the good times" is a placeholder for scenes the text never wrote; grief is asserted before it is earned.

**Strong.**
> At the funeral Ilan could not find the right place to stand. His brother had always stood at the back, near the door, because he hated being looked at.
> Ilan took the back. Nobody noticed.
> Later, when the mourners had gone, he found the two cups his brother kept behind the stove for the nights they argued past midnight. One of them still had tea in it.
> He poured it out, slowly, and put the cup back exactly where it had been.

**Why it works.** Grief appears as behaviour: standing where the brother stood, the two cups, the refusal to move anything. The reader supplies the feeling, which makes it stronger and more specific. One concrete detail (tea still in the cup) does the work of a paragraph of lament.

**Rule.** Invest before you spend; let behaviour carry the emotion.

## 8. Repetitive scene shape vs varied unit

**Weak (three consecutive chapters).**
> Ch. 4: Tovan arrived at the court, was underestimated, and demonstrated his skill.
> Ch. 5: Tovan arrived at the guild, was underestimated, and demonstrated his skill.
> Ch. 6: Tovan arrived at the barracks, was underestimated, and demonstrated his skill.

**Why it fails.** The shape is identical; only nouns change. The reader learns the pattern and skims.

**Strong (same three beats, varied units).**
> Ch. 4: Tovan arrives at court and is underestimated (performance).
> Ch. 5: Tovan must sit through the guild's audit of his past, defending a lie he told in ch. 2 (procedure as pressure).
> Ch. 6: Tovan loses. The barracks scene is the aftermath: he is reassigned, and the person who vouched for him is demoted (failure and consequence).

**Why it works.** Each unit does a different job: display, exposure, cost. The third chapter uses the pattern's absence to raise stakes, which makes the next performance mean something.

**Rule.** Rotate scene types; vary stakes, not costumes.

## 9. Continuity slip vs ledgered correction

**Weak.**
> Ch. 2: The scar ran from his left eyebrow to his jaw.
> Ch. 9: She traced the scar on his right cheek and asked how he got it.

**Why it fails.** A small factual break accumulates into distrust. Readers who notice stop investing in details, because details are no longer reliable.

**Strong.**
> Ch. 9: She reached for the scar on his jaw, and he turned his head so she would touch the other side.
> "It's on the left," she said.
> "It was," he said. "The surgeons moved it when they rebuilt the cheek. They did a good job. I paid for good."

**Why it works.** The discrepancy is converted into character and world information (surgery, money, vanity, control). The ledger flags the conflict; the scene metabolises it.

**Rule.** When new text contradicts old, either earliest mention wins or the text dramatises the correction.

## 10. Ending that resolves events vs ending that answers the question

**Weak ending.**
> The dark lord was defeated, the kingdom was saved, and everyone celebrated. Lira was crowned queen and ruled wisely for many years.

**Why it fails.** Events are resolved; the central question (was Lira willing to become the thing she fought?) is not answered. The ending could belong to any book in the genre.

**Strong ending.**
> The dark lord was dead. The crown was on the table between them, and Lira found that she was not looking at it.
> "You'll need someone to hold the north," she said.
> "You were going to take the crown."
> "I was going to take the north." She pushed the crown toward him. "I know what I am. I am not going to pretend otherwise in front of a court."
> He picked it up. She walked out before he could thank her, because she knew that if he thanked her she would stay, and she had already decided what staying would cost.
> Outside, the snow had stopped. She had not expected that.

**Why it works.** The final choice answers the question through action and cost: she refuses the role that would have made her the thing she feared, and the refusal is a loss, not a triumph. The last image rhymes with the opening's weather and changes it (snow stopping = an ending she did not plan).

**Rule.** Answer the central question with a choice that costs; make the final image rhyme and change.

## Using these examples

- In DRAFT mode, pick the pair that matches the problem you are solving and apply the mechanism, not the surface.
- In DIAGNOSE mode, name which mechanism is missing and cite the writer's own text.
- In REVISE mode, rewrite only the failing unit; do not import the example's voice.
